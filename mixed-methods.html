Discord Voice and Offline Social Functioning in Socially Anxious Adults: A Longitudinal Mixed-Methods Study

My PhD research examines how speaking in Discord voice channels may shape offline social confidence and engagement among adults with social anxiety. Rather than treating time spent online as a single measure, the thesis focuses specifically on active spoken participation in live voice channels—a form of interaction that sits between text-based communication and face-to-face conversation.

The central question is whether Discord voice can function as a bridge to offline socialising. Repeated voice interaction may provide a manageable environment in which users practise spontaneity, conversational timing, joining groups, and coping with awkwardness. These experiences may strengthen confidence in handling face-to-face interaction and make voluntary offline social engagement more likely.

However, the research also considers alternative patterns. Discord voice participation may not transfer into offline life, may become a safer substitute for in-person contact, or may compensate for situations in which offline social opportunities are limited.

The project consists of three linked studies. The first develops and refines the key concepts through qualitative interviews and pilot testing. The second tests the proposed pathway through a four-wave longitudinal survey. The third uses follow-up interviews to explore how participants understand experiences of transfer, non-transfer, substitution, and compensation.

By combining psychological theory with the lived experiences of Discord users, the thesis aims to provide a more nuanced account of how online voice environments can support—or sometimes constrain—offline social life.
