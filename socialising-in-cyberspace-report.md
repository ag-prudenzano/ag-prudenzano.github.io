# Socialising in Cyberspace: A Phenomenological Interview Study on the Experiences of Discord Users

## Abstract

Social media platforms have become important channels of communication and social interaction. Although substantial psychological research has examined platforms such as Facebook and Instagram, comparatively little research has explored users’ experiences of socialising on Discord. Discord differs from many other social media platforms through its combination of server-based communities and text, voice, and video communication. This study explored people’s lived experiences of socialising on Discord using a phenomenological approach. Two Discord users participated in unstructured interviews designed to elicit detailed descriptions of their experiences. Interview transcripts were analysed thematically within a phenomenological framework, with attention to intentionality, reduction, description, and essence, as well as the lifeworld dimensions of temporality, spatiality, embodiment, and intersubjectivity. Eight themes were identified: Meeting Social Needs, Authentic Relationships, Support with Daily Life, Exploration and Discovery, Too Many People, Interpersonal Conflict, Self-Awareness and Behaviour, and a New Way to Socialise. Participants described Discord as a means of maintaining a social life during periods of isolation, developing meaningful relationships, learning languages, exchanging information, and interacting with people from different cultural backgrounds. They also reported challenges associated with crowded online spaces, interpersonal conflict, and increased awareness of their own behaviour. These findings provide an exploratory qualitative account of socialising on Discord and identify areas for further qualitative and quantitative cyberpsychology research.

## Introduction and Literature Review

Social media refers to a range of interactive, web-based technologies that enable people to communicate and exchange information with others (Boyd & Ellison, 2007, as cited in Ryan et al., 2017). Forms of social media include instant-messaging applications such as WhatsApp and Telegram; social-networking platforms such as Facebook, Instagram, and Discord; blogging and microblogging platforms such as Tumblr, Medium, and Steemit; multiplayer online games such as World of Warcraft and Call of Duty; and virtual worlds such as Second Life (Ryan et al., 2017). The number of social media users worldwide increased from 2.86 billion in 2017 to 3.78 billion in 2021 and was predicted to reach 4.41 billion by 2025 (Statista Research Department, 2021).

The increasing use of these relatively new platforms, together with their possible effects on mental health, has become an important topic of discussion and research among scientists, governments, and technology companies. Social media may be particularly useful for maintaining social connectedness during periods of restricted face-to-face contact, such as the global lockdowns introduced during the COVID-19 pandemic (Barreda-Ángeles & Hartmann, 2022). During this period, the average time spent on social media by users in the United States increased from 54 to 65 minutes per day (Statista Research Department, 2021).

Researchers have also suggested that online communication platforms may help reduce loneliness among people who are socially isolated or have limited mobility (Caplan, 2006; Lee et al., 2012; Saunders & Chester, 2008, as cited in Ryan et al., 2017). For people who experience social anxiety, the relative anonymity of online communication may provide a more comfortable environment for interaction and self-expression (Bargh et al., 2002; Suler, 2005, as cited in Ryan et al., 2017).

The growth of internet use has also contributed to the formation of virtual communities (Fernback, 1999; Hiltz & Wellman, 1997, as cited in Koh & Kim, 2004). These communities can be defined as groups of individuals who share common interests and interact within a shared digital space (Agostini & Mechant, 2019). Members of virtual communities may have opportunities to socialise, experience a sense of belonging, fulfil interpersonal needs, and form emotional connections with others (Blanchard & Markus, 2004; Koh & Kim, 2003, as cited in Hatun & Demirci, 2020).

However, research has also identified possible disadvantages associated with social media use. Ryan et al. (2017) reviewed evidence concerning the positive and negative outcomes of social media use in relation to social capital, sense of community, and loneliness. Overall, the findings suggested that social media can help people form friendships and communities, reduce loneliness, and increase social capital. Nevertheless, some users may experience weaker offline relationships or cyberostracism. Cyberostracism refers to the experience of feeling ignored or excluded online, particularly when expected feedback or responses from other users are not received (Williams et al., 2000, as cited in Ryan et al., 2017).

Some studies have also found an association between social media use and increased loneliness. For example, Stepanikova et al. (2010, as cited in Ryan et al., 2017) conducted a quantitative study involving 754 adults and found that participants who spent more time communicating online reported greater loneliness than those who used online communication less frequently or not at all. Time spent communicating online was positively correlated with loneliness and negatively correlated with overall life satisfaction.

These findings do not necessarily demonstrate that social media use causes loneliness. It is also possible that people who already feel lonely use social media more frequently, or that other factors influence both social media use and feelings of loneliness. Nevertheless, some researchers have argued that online social interaction may create an impression of friendship without providing the physical presence and intimacy associated with face-to-face relationships (Turkle, 2011, as cited in Ryan et al., 2017).

The apparently contradictory findings may partly reflect differences in the ways people use social media. For example, communicating directly with supportive friends is likely to be different from passively browsing other users’ content or experiencing exclusion from an online group. The type and quality of social media use may therefore be more informative than the amount of time spent online. Social media may support connection and belonging in some circumstances while contributing to comparison, exclusion, or loneliness in others.

Hjetland et al. (2021) argued that the relationship between social media use and mental health had not been studied sufficiently. They also noted that the overall quality of the existing evidence was low, according to a review by Webster et al. (2021, as cited in Hjetland et al., 2021). Most previous studies had used quantitative methods. Although these studies identified positive correlations between time spent on social media and mental health outcomes such as depression and anxiety, the associations were generally weak and of limited practical significance.

Quantitative studies can identify patterns and relationships between variables, but they may not explain how or why particular experiences occur. Researchers have therefore suggested that more qualitative studies are needed to examine the processes that may explain associations between social media use and mental health (Keles et al., 2020, as cited in Hjetland et al., 2021). Qualitative methods allow researchers to explore individual experiences in greater depth and may help to explain previous quantitative findings. They may also provide insight into users’ perspectives on social media policies and regulation (Hjetland et al., 2021).

Existing qualitative findings demonstrate that users can experience social media in different and sometimes contradictory ways. Some participants have described social media as having a negative influence on their mental health (O’Reilly et al., 2018, as cited in Hjetland et al., 2021). Other studies have found that users believe social media affects them both positively and negatively (O’Reilly et al., 2020; Throuvala et al., 2019; Weinstein et al., 2018, as cited in Hjetland et al., 2021).

For example, Weinstein et al. (2018, as cited in Hjetland et al., 2021) found that activities such as browsing could produce both admiration and envy, while interacting with other users could generate feelings of both connection and loneliness. These findings indicate that the effects of social media may depend on the activity being performed, the context in which it occurs, and the individual user’s interpretation of the experience.

Hjetland et al. (2021) investigated adolescents’ lived experiences of social media use through interviews conducted across five focus groups. Participants reported that social media changed how people communicated and related to one another in both positive and negative ways. Positive experiences included social support and a sense of belonging. Negative experiences included perceived stress, pressure concerning physical appearance, reduced sleep, procrastination with homework, and the perception that people behaved more negatively online than they would during face-to-face interactions.

Previous research has also explored people’s motivations for using social media. Throuvala et al. (2019) conducted a qualitative study involving six focus groups to examine the motives underlying social media use. A need for control emerged as a central motivation and appeared to be reinforced by fear of missing out and nomophobia, which refers to the fear of losing mobile-phone connectivity. The authors suggested that these factors may contribute to increased engagement and, in some cases, compulsive social media use.

Discord is a group-chatting application that was initially developed primarily for gamers but has since become a platform for broader forms of social interaction. It allows users to join or create communities known as servers. Each server has its own members, topics, rules, and communication channels. According to figures published on Discord’s official website, the platform had 150 million monthly active users, 19 million active servers per week, and four billion minutes of server conversations each day in 2021 (Discord, 2021).

One of Discord’s commonly used features is voice chat, which allows users to communicate with one another in real time. This feature distinguishes Discord from platforms such as Reddit and Facebook, where interaction is more commonly based on written posts and comments. Discord also allows users to participate in persistent online communities organised around shared interests, activities, or relationships.

At the time of the literature search, no qualitative studies were identified that specifically examined the relationship between Discord use and mental health or explored users’ individual social experiences on the platform. This represented a gap in the literature because Discord’s combination of community-based servers, text communication, and real-time voice interaction may produce social experiences that differ from those associated with other social media platforms.

The present study therefore aimed to explore people’s experiences of using Discord, with a particular focus on its social aspects. The study was guided by the following research question:

How do users describe their social experiences on Discord?

## Method

### Design

This exploratory qualitative study used a descriptive phenomenological approach to investigate the lived experiences of people who regularly socialised on Discord. A phenomenological design was appropriate because the research question concerned how participants experienced and understood social interaction on the platform, rather than the measurement of predetermined variables. The study was informed by the Husserlian principles of phenomenological reduction, horizontalisation, and imaginative variation. The interview transcripts were examined using an inductive, phenomenologically informed thematic analysis based on Lynden (2016), with particular attention to the lifeworld dimensions of temporality, spatiality, embodiment, and intersubjectivity.

### Participants and Recruitment

Two Discord users participated in the study. Participants were required to be aged 18 or over, use Discord regularly, and participate regularly in Discord server voice channels. The recruitment materials also specified that participants should not belong to a group identified as vulnerable within the study protocol. Both participants reported socialising on Discord approximately every day.

Purposive sampling was used to recruit participants with direct and sustained experience of the phenomenon under investigation. Participants were recruited from two Discord servers of which the researcher was also a member. The researcher was acquainted with both participants before recruitment. Each participant was approached individually through Discord’s private-messaging system and provided with a participant information leaflet and consent form. Subsequent correspondence also took place through private messages.

The small sample was consistent with the study’s exploratory and idiographic emphasis. It was intended to permit detailed examination of individual accounts rather than establish representativeness or make claims about Discord users generally.

### Data Collection

Data were collected through individual, audio-only interviews conducted using Zoom. The researcher and each participant joined the interview from their respective homes and used headphones with microphones. Participants were informed in advance that an interview could last up to one hour.

Although initially conceived as unstructured, the interviews were conducted in a loosely semi-structured and conversational manner. Open-ended questions were used to invite descriptions of participants’ social experiences, while follow-up questions were adapted in response to each account. Questions addressed matters such as how authentic socialising on Discord felt, how Discord relationships differed from face-to-face relationships, how socialising on the platform affected participants’ daily lives, and what role Discord played in their lives. Examples included: “How do you feel when you are not on Discord?”, “How does socialising on Discord affect your daily life at home?”, and “What role does Discord play in your life?”

The first interview also functioned as a pilot. It helped the researcher identify difficulties in sustaining an open-ended interview and develop a wider selection of prompts for the second interview. The pilot interview was retained in the final dataset and analysed as Interview 01 because it contained material relevant to the research question. Consequently, the two interviews differed somewhat in structure: the first relied more heavily on direct questions, whereas the second developed into a longer and more conversational exchange.

During the interviews, the researcher sometimes referred to their own Discord experiences. This self-disclosure was used to establish rapport, sustain the conversation, and encourage participants to elaborate on their experiences. Because these contributions may also have influenced the direction and content of participants’ responses, the interview accounts were understood as having been produced through interaction between the researcher and participant.

With participants’ consent, the interviews were audio-recorded using Zoom’s recording function. The recordings were transcribed initially using Otter.ai. Because the automated output contained inaccuracies, the researcher manually corrected the transcripts using a text editor. Identifying information, including usernames, server names, dates of birth, and locations, was removed or obscured. Participants were identified in the transcripts and report as Participant 01 and Participant 02.

### Data Analysis

The researcher analysed the transcripts inductively using the phenomenologically informed thematic procedure described by Lynden (2016). Coding and theme development were documented separately for each interview before the two accounts were compared. The analysis involved the following stages:

1. Familiarisation: Each transcript was read repeatedly to develop familiarity with the participant’s account as a whole.
2. Horizontalisation: Statements, phrases, and metaphors relevant to the experience of socialising on Discord were identified. At this initial stage, potentially relevant extracts were considered without assigning premature importance to particular statements.
3. Initial code development: Each selected extract was summarised to identify the experience or meaning it appeared to communicate.
4. Final code development: Initial interpretations were reviewed and refined into more concise final codes describing what the extracts revealed about the participant’s experience.
5. Theme development: Related final codes were grouped into candidate themes. Themes were labelled to capture the shared experiential feature represented by the grouped codes.
6. Lifeworld interpretation: The themes and relevant extracts were considered in relation to temporality, spatiality, embodiment, and intersubjectivity. Imaginative variation was used to examine possible meanings from different perspectives and distinguish central experiential features from incidental ones.
7. Comparison across accounts: Each interview was first treated as an individual case. Themes and codes were then compared across the two transcripts to identify similarities and differences between participants’ experiences.

Coding was interpretative rather than determined solely by the frequency with which an experience was mentioned. Codes that were relevant to the research question but did not fit a broader theme were retained as ungrouped findings rather than discarded. The coding record therefore preserved a visible progression from interview extracts to initial codes, final codes, themes, and lifeworld interpretations.

### Reflexivity

The researcher was a regular Discord user and was acquainted with both participants before data collection. This familiarity supported rapport and helped the researcher understand platform-specific language and practices. However, it also created a risk that the researcher’s assumptions and experiences could influence the questions asked, the participants’ responses, and the subsequent interpretation of the data.

The researcher attempted phenomenological reduction by identifying and setting aside prior assumptions while attending closely to participants’ descriptions. Complete detachment was not assumed, particularly because the researcher sometimes shared personal experiences and introduced interpretations during the interviews. The resulting accounts were therefore understood as interactionally produced rather than as entirely independent descriptions. The researcher’s familiarity with Discord and prior relationships with the participants were considered when interpreting the findings and are acknowledged as limitations of the study.

### Ethical Considerations

The study was conducted as part of the Open University module DE300, *Investigating Psychology 3*. Before participation, each participant received an information leaflet, a data-protection privacy notice, and an informed-consent form. Consent was obtained by email before interview arrangements were finalised. Participants confirmed that they were aged 18 or over, understood the purpose and procedures of the research, consented to audio recording, and permitted anonymised quotations to be used in the research report and any subsequent publications.

Participation was voluntary. Participants were informed that they could decline to answer any question, stop the interview, or withdraw without providing a reason or incurring a penalty. They could request the withdrawal of their data until 1 April 2022. After that date, withdrawal could not be guaranteed because the interviews were expected to have been transcribed, anonymised, and incorporated into the analysis. Participants were briefed before and debriefed after their interviews.

The interviews were not designed to request special-category or otherwise sensitive personal information. Nevertheless, participants could voluntarily introduce experiences that they regarded as relevant. The information leaflet warned that some questions might cause discomfort and explained that participants could skip questions or end the interview. Direct questions about cyberbullying and cyberostracism were avoided, although related experiences introduced voluntarily by participants were retained where relevant.

The researcher knew the participants’ identities, so complete anonymity could not be provided during data collection. Confidentiality was instead protected by removing names and other directly or indirectly identifying information from the transcripts. Server names were obscured, participants were assigned numerical identifiers, and only anonymised quotations were used in the report. Consent records were stored separately from the anonymised transcripts.

Recordings and consent forms were to be retained for no longer than one year, while anonymised transcripts could be retained for longer. Research data were stored on the researcher’s password-protected hard drive, with a backup on an encrypted or password-protected external drive. Data could be accessed by the researcher and supervisor and, in exceptional circumstances, authorised members of the DE300 module team. With the exception of correspondence retained as evidence of consent, email communications were to be destroyed after data collection.

The participant information materials disclosed that Zoom would process limited electronic information associated with the interviews. They did not, however, specifically identify Otter.ai as an external transcription service. The use of Otter.ai should therefore be recognised as a limitation in the transparency of the original data-processing information.

## Results

In general, social experiences on Discord were described as a new way to make friends and exchange information with different people. Descriptions from the two interviews both had their similarities and differences and lifeworld experiences were described in various ways. ## Discussion

Overall, socialising on Discord was a multifaceted experience with both negative and positive aspects, but mainly, the participants described it as a platform where they could fulfil their social needs in times of isolation or exchange information with other users about something they are interested in. The lack of a physical presence while socialising was both described as a facilitator for less restrained conversations and as an obstacle for mutual understanding due to the absence of body language. Both participants saw the use of webcams in voice channels as something personal, for example, the first participant saw it as an act of trust, while the second participant didn't feel comfortable about showing themselves on camera.

The findings of the present study reflect those of Barreda-Ángeles and Hartmann (2022), in that social media can be a tool for staying socialised during lockdown periods or alleviate loneliness in other situations of isolation as mentioned by other researchers (Caplan, 2006; Lee, Cheung & Thadani, 2012; Saunders & Chester, 2008, cited in Ryan et al., 2017). As the first participant has said, the anonymous nature of Discord can create a comfortable place for more authentic relationships and self-expression, which also reflects the findings of previous research done on other social media (Bargh, McKenna & Fitzsimons, 2002; Suler, 2005, cited in Ryan et al., 2017). Previous studies have also looked into the category of virtual communities, which Discord certainly fits into as it was mainly built for gamers. Similarly to the findings of the current study, it was reported that members of such communities can benefit from fulfilling their social needs and finding likeminded people (Blanchard & Markus 2004; Koh & Kim, 2003, cited in Hatun & Demirci, 2020). Results of the present study also reflect those of a recent review article on the positive and negative outcomes of social media usage (Ryan et al., 2017), where it was indicated that social media use can help form friendships and reduce loneliness, however, the article also mentioned a risk for weaker offline relationships as a potential drawback, which was not observed in the current study, and a risk of exposure to cyberostracism, which was not covered due to ethical issues.

In contrast, findings from the current study contradict those of Stepanikova and colleagues (2010, cited in Ryan et al., 2017), where social media usage was found to be positively correlated with loneliness, meanwhile the present study's participants described Discord as a tool to alleviate loneliness. However, these are observations from only two individuals compared to those of a quantitative study of 754 participants. According to the first participant's descriptions in regards to the authenticity of relationships on Discord, the lack of a physical presence was described as an advantage for users to safely express and be themselves, which contradicts the argument made by Turkle (2011, cited in Ryan et al., 2017), where they stated that online friendships are illusory due to a lack of a physical presence. This could be explained by the fact that users on Discord mainly interact in voice channels and use webcams, where the 'physical presence' is more present. Considering that Discord is essentially a platform with a format of communication which differs from that of other social media platforms such as Reddit, Facebook, Instagram or Twitter, the social experience is expected to be different too. However, it appears that on both types of platforms, users tend and have the possibility to behave worse than in real life, as was observed in the qualitative study by Hjetland and colleagues (2021). As for the procrastination of homework or sleep disruption mentioned in the same study, the findings from the present research suggest that socialising on Discord was described as an experience which can increase productivity levels, as mentioned in the third theme by the first participant.

The current study had its limitations in several aspects. First, the researcher's own experience in being a regular Discord user could have affected the way the participants' accounts were interpreted during code development. At the same time, this could also have been an advantage as the researcher was able to relate better to what the participants were describing and could support the conversations by sharing their own experience. Second, because the nature of the current study was qualitative there is always a risk of bias on the part of the researcher since the process involved recording and analysing subjective experiences which are not directly observable. Third, it is likely that the experiences described by the two participants do not apply for the millions of other people who use Discord, thus, further research with more recruits are needed to identify occurring patterns which can be then used for developing quantitative investigations. Since no previous research was found on Discord usage it was difficult to make comparisons of the current findings with research done on social media platforms which are essentially different, hence it is important to note that the experience of socialising on Facebook is most likely not the same as socialising on Discord. However, behaviours such as trolling can be observed across both platforms since they both provide a certain level of anonymity. As this is one of the first, if not the only, qualitative research conducted on the social experiences of Discord users, further research needs to be done with more participants. Also, because the negative aspects of Discord usage were overlooked due to ethical issues, future studies need to take into account how Discord usage can possibly be a negative experience by considering such phenomena as cyberbullying or cyberostracism.
